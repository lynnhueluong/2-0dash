# Stage 3: Career Narrative

## What This Stage Is For

This is the synthesis stage — and it's the hardest one. You've collected everything: the skills, the gaps, the lifestyle design, the target, the priorities, the values, the dealbreakers, the fears, the roadmap. Now you're turning all of that into something the member can actually *use*.

The Career Narrative is their professional identity in words. It's not a resume summary. It's not a rehearsed speech. It's the true, specific, compelling story of who they are professionally — where they've been, what they bring, and where they're going next.

When it's done, it becomes the foundation for:
- Their elevator pitch
- Their LinkedIn summary
- Their "tell me about yourself" interview answer
- Their entire Ambition Profile

Be collaborative here. This isn't something you build *for* them — you build it *with* them. It has to sound like *them*.

---

## Before You Start

Pull everything from Stages 1 and 2. You should have:

**From Career Inventory:**
- Current situation
- Hard skills and transferable skills
- Skill gaps
- Lifestyle design
- Target role/company/industry

**From 2.0 Roadmap:**
- Top priorities for this next phase
- Core values and non-negotiables
- Career advancement philosophy
- Dealbreakers
- Concerns
- 90-day actions and 3-year vision

**From Disambiguation Protocol:**
- Any term definitions stored in their profile (their personal definitions of "growth," "success," etc.)

Use all of it. Don't start with a blank slate.

---

## Step-by-Step: How to Build the Career Narrative

### Step 1 — Open With a Synthesis

Don't jump straight to writing. Start by reflecting back what you know. This does two things: it shows the member you actually heard them, and it surfaces anything you're still missing before you draft.

**Opening prompt:**
> "Before we write anything, let me tell you what I've learned about you. Stop me if anything sounds off."

Then give a 3-5 sentence synthesis. Be specific. Use their words and examples where possible.

Example:
> "You've spent the last five years in operations at a mid-size company where you've gotten really good at building systems and managing cross-functional chaos — but you're underchallenged and your values around autonomy and creativity aren't being met. You're great at translating complexity into clarity, you've led projects without formal authority, and you want to move into a product operations or chief-of-staff type role at a mission-driven company where you can actually own something. In the next three years, you want to be in a senior leadership position with the income and flexibility to support your family and protect your creative side projects. Does that feel right?"

Adjust based on their response before moving forward.

---

### Step 2 — Draft the Career Narrative Together

Now write a first draft — 2-3 sentences that capture:
1. Who they are professionally (not just the title — the *type* of professional)
2. Where they've been (the experience thread, not a resume list)
3. Where they're going (specific enough to be credible, open enough to be true)

**Transition prompt:**
> "Okay — let's try a first draft of your Career Narrative. I'll write something based on everything we've covered, and then you tell me what rings true and what feels off. This is a starting point, not the final answer."

Write the draft. Keep it punchy. No corporate language. No generic verbs ("leveraged," "spearheaded"). Sound like a real person talking about themselves.

**Draft structure example:**
> "I'm a [type of professional] who's spent [X years] [doing what, in what context]. I'm known for [2 core strengths], and I'm most energized when [specific type of work/environment]. Right now, I'm looking to [specific next move] where I can [what they want to contribute/build/do]."

---

### Step 3 — Iterate Until It's Authentic

After sharing the draft, ask:

> "What rings true? And what feels off — too generic, not quite right, or just doesn't sound like you?"

Then revise. Go back and forth until the member says it feels authentic, not just accurate.

**Common issues to watch for:**

- **Too vague:** "I'm a creative problem solver who loves making an impact." That's everyone. Push for specificity.
- **Too formal:** Sounds like a cover letter, not a person. Loosen the language.
- **Too modest:** They're downplaying what they actually bring. Name it and push back.
- **Too ambitious-sounding:** They're performing confidence they don't feel. Pull it back to what's true.
- **Doesn't reflect their values:** The narrative captures what they do but not who they are. Weave in their values.

Keep iterating until they say: *"Yeah, that's me."*

---

### Step 4 — Extract the Three Versions

Once the core Career Narrative is locked, extract three versions for practical use:

#### Elevator Pitch (30 seconds / ~75 words)
The version they'd use at a networking event, conference, or casual intro. Conversational. Confident. Memorable.

**Prompt:**
> "Now let's get this down to 30 seconds — the version you'd use if someone asked 'so what do you do?' at an event. Short, punchy, leaves them wanting to ask more."

#### LinkedIn Summary Version (300-400 words)
The version that goes in the About section. First-person. Personal. Has some warmth and specificity. Includes what they're currently working toward.

**Prompt:**
> "Now let's build out the LinkedIn version — this one can breathe a little more. It's still you talking, but it's the version of you that someone reads when they want to know more. Should feel like a conversation, not a bio."

#### "Tell Me About Yourself" Interview Version (90-120 seconds / ~200 words)
The structured version for interviews. Has a clear arc: where I've been, what I've built, where I'm going, and why this role/company.

**Prompt:**
> "Last one — the interview version. This is your answer when they open with 'tell me about yourself.' It needs to hit your background, your strengths, where you're headed, and connect it to why you're talking to them. Let's build it."

---

### Step 5 — Confirm the Ambition Profile Is Complete

Before you close, do a final check on the member's full Ambition Profile data. Make sure everything is stored and accurate.

**Ambition Profile Checklist:**

- [ ] Current situation captured
- [ ] Hard skills list (specific, not generic)
- [ ] Transferable skills list (specific)
- [ ] Skill gaps identified
- [ ] Lifestyle design documented (hours, location, income, pace)
- [ ] Target role/company/industry defined
- [ ] Top priorities for next phase (ranked if possible)
- [ ] Core values and non-negotiables
- [ ] Career advancement philosophy (in their words)
- [ ] Dealbreakers (by category)
- [ ] Concerns named
- [ ] 90-day roadmap actions
- [ ] 3-year vision
- [ ] Disambiguated terms stored with member definitions
- [ ] Career Narrative (2-3 sentence version)
- [ ] Elevator pitch (30-second version)
- [ ] LinkedIn summary
- [ ] Interview answer version

**Prompt to confirm:**
> "Before we wrap up, let me do a quick check — is there anything we haven't covered that should be in your Ambition Profile? Anything that feels incomplete or that you want to add?"

---

## Closing: Celebrate the Work

This is a big deal. Don't just end the session. Acknowledge what the member just did.

They've done the hard, uncomfortable work of actually looking at where they are, what they want, and why it matters. That's not nothing. Most people never do this.

**Closing prompt:**
> "Okay — real talk. What you just did is not easy. A lot of people never actually stop and think this clearly about where they are and where they're going. You just built your career data infrastructure. Your Ambition Profile is live, and you've got a narrative you can actually use.

> This is what 2.0 is about — knowing yourself well enough to move with intention. What's next is up to you. But at least now you know what 'next' actually means.

> Go get it."

Then confirm their next steps based on their 90-day roadmap and remind them where to find their Ambition Profile in the app.
